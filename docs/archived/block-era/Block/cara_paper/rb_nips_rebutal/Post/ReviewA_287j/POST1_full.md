## POST 1 — Response to Reviewer 287j (writing · clarity · batching)

We sincerely thank the reviewer for their time and careful reading! We appreciate the recognition that "the paper aims to solve the right problem and probably does a good job in solving it" — the presentation issues you raise are real, and we resolve each one below with precise wording that we commit to in the revision.

**Relation to LLM-D / AIBrix (your summary).** LLM-D and AIBrix provide serving infrastructure with routing and load-balancing components; to our knowledge neither fuses per-request quality–latency–cost scoring into a single assignment over heterogeneous concrete instances, which — together with the controlled isolation of that fusion boundary (reported in our reply to Reviewer gTfM) — is our contribution.

**Q1: Batching vs continuous batching**

> "you schedule in batches. I suppose that means that you do not use continuous-batching? > ... Does that mean that you intentionally delay a request that arrived, still use > continuous batching, but schedule only when you reach the required batch size/time?"

Your last reading is exactly right, with one important correction: there is no *required* batch size. There are two batching layers, and we will reserve distinct terms for them in the revision: (i) engine-level continuous batching — each vLLM instance composes decode batches per iteration (the serving substrate, unchanged by our system; once dispatched, a request immediately enters its instance's continuous-batching loop); (ii) scheduler-level request batching ("scheduling round") — our adaptive batch-formation window groups concurrently-arriving requests so one gathered prediction round scores the full request×instance candidate matrix. Requests are never held for a mandatory minimum batch size: the scheduler fires when either the adaptive size limit is reached or a timeout expires, with the timer starting at the first arrival — a lone request waits at most the timeout, and an "uneven" (small) batch simply fires at the timeout. All formation delay is charged in the client-observed end-to-end latency we report.

**Why batch at all — the design rationale behind Q1.** Routers in the compared systems decide one request at a time; forming a scheduling round buys three things they cannot have. (i) It avoids herding: within a round each dispatch immediately updates a local view of the chosen instance's state (dead-reckoning), so later requests do not all pile onto whichever instance looked idle before any of them were placed. (ii) It amortizes the fixed cost of a quality/latency-aware decision over the whole batch, which is why reported decision compute *falls* as load rises (44.7 ms at λ=6 to 25.7 ms at λ=24) rather than growing. (iii) It gives the competing signals a common range — quality, cost and latency are min–max normalized across the candidates present in that round, which is what makes one weighted score meaningful across heterogeneous tiers. This also motivates making the size limit adaptive rather than fixed (Q2): the batch grows and the timeout lengthens in the busy regime where those benefits are largest, and collapses to a small batch and short timeout at low load. The formation wait is charged in full in every reported end-to-end number.

**Q2: The adaptive size-limit mechanism**

> "besides the vague sentence ... you never discuss how this mechanism works?"

Agreed — the mechanism is concrete and we will state it: the adaptive limit is a busy-instance-ratio step schedule, where the busy-instance ratio is the fraction of instances with at least one waiting request: ratio < 0.3 → (batch 4, 50 ms timeout); < 0.7 → (16, 100 ms); otherwise (32, 200 ms). Our ablations bound each piece: disabling adaptivity costs 5.1–6.9% end-to-end latency, and batch-size-1 forfeits amortization entirely and collapses under load on the submitted implementation.

**Q3: Model choice vs KV-cache hit-rate and TTFT**

> "how can the model choice affect the KV-Cache hit-rate? How much does it affects the > TTFT ...?"

On prefix caching: each request is a single turn — where a source corpus is multi-turn we take the first turn only, the standard preparation in the vLLM benchmark harness — so consecutive requests share almost no prefix, and prefix caching is configured identically for every system since all run the same serving path — so it cannot advantage RouteBalance. We did not run a with/without ablation. Our target here is decode-dominated end-to-end latency; workloads where prefix reuse is central (retrieval-augmented or genuinely multi-turn serving, where a conversation's history is replayed) are a different regime that we leave to future work. Model choice affects KV pressure through model size and sequence lengths. KV occupancy is not a term in the assignment objective, though KV utilization and free-block count are among the instance-state features the latency predictor reads, so they reach the decision through the predicted TPOT. On TTFT: the online assignment objective is decode-dominated (learned per-(model, GPU) TPOT × queue-aware decode iterations) and carries no TTFT term — the current phrasing will be corrected accordingly. Extending the objective to TTFT, together with a prefix-affinity term, is future work (see also our reply to Reviewer gTfM, Q2).
